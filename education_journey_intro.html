Building the Next Skill Set the Same Way I Built RouteToServe

No bootcamp, no employer sponsoring it — just a structured plan, real discipline, and a real production app to learn against.

Why This Exists

Naming the gap honestly

I'm strong on orchestration and product judgment — directing an AI coding agent, shipping real production features on RouteToServe. What I was shaky on was being able to explain, unprompted, why an AI system did what it did — across the technical layer, the business layer, and the AI-specific layer. That's the actual gap between "gets good work done with AI help" and "is the credible person a company hands an ambiguous problem to."

So instead of waiting for someone else to hand me a curriculum, I built one — the same way I've approached everything else: name the gap precisely, source real material, hold myself to a real cadence, and produce artifacts, not certificates.

The Approach

Six pillars, RouteToServe as the anchor

Two of the six pillars — technical foundation and eval literacy — get learned directly against RTS's own codebase and data, not a generic tutorial dataset. The other four fill genuine gaps. Two timeframes: a six-week orientation sprint first (know the map, not master it), then ongoing depth over the following year, one pillar at a time.

1
AI literacy & tool fluency

Prompt engineering, workflow automation, evaluating AI output — already daily-practiced on RTS. This pillar mostly formalizes what I already do.

2
Agentic systems fluency

How agents, tool use, and orchestration actually work under the hood — well enough to explain "why did the AI do that" on the spot. The pillar with the biggest real gap going in.

3
Technical foundation

Python and SQL, via MIT OCW's 6.100L and 6.0002 — SQL applied directly to RTS's own database schema, not a toy dataset.

4
Business/domain translation

Scoping ambiguous problems, framing technical work in terms stakeholders actually care about — revenue, risk, efficiency.

5
Deployment/production literacy

How software actually ships — the layer between "works on my machine" and "works for real users," which RTS's own production incidents have made concrete.

6
Evals & quality-checking

Building a real eval against RTS's own OCR scan-in pipeline — deterministic scorers plus an LLM-as-judge, validated against a hand-labeled set.

The North Star vs. the Real Plan

Two destinations, kept deliberately separate

It would've been easy to write one plan and call it done. I split it into two on purpose, because they require genuinely different things and conflating them wastes both.

🌟 North star (aspirational, multi-year)

Elite Forward Deployed Engineer roles — Palantir, OpenAI, Scale AI tier. Real hands-on coding and infrastructure depth, T-shaped: deep engineering plus broad range. Requires actually being the builder, not just the director of AI. This is where ambition points, not where next week's plan lives.

🎯 Real plan (the actual near-term target)

AI Implementation Specialist / Agentic Workflow Designer tier — business-fluent, technically literate enough to direct, interpret, and quality-check AI output, and explain the decisions to stakeholders. This is the profile I already have real evidence for, from RouteToServe itself.

The honest tension: my own instinct going in was closer to "I don't need to know how to do things the traditional way, I just need to know how to direct someone who can" — which describes the real plan accurately, but directly contradicts what the north star actually requires. Rather than pretend that tension away, I named it and resolved it on paper: the north star stays the north star, informing stretch goals and keeping ambition honest, without quietly becoming the operating plan and burning the realistic timeline.

The Six-Week Sprint

Orientation first, depth later — deliberately in that order

The sprint's job isn't mastery. It's knowing the map — what each pillar actually requires, where the real gaps sit, and which pillars can lean on work already being done on RouteToServe. Depth comes after, over the following year, once the sequencing decision is actually informed instead of guessed at.

W1
Orientation + Pillar 1 formalize + Pillar 3 confirm

Name what I already do (AI/tool fluency), confirm what I already know (technical foundation), take the first bite of the biggest gap (agentic systems).

W2
Pillar 2 core: MCP + Claude API

Understand the protocol I already use daily well enough to explain it, plus the heaviest single technical course in the sprint.

W3
Pillar 2 continued: agent patterns + orchestration

Multi-agent patterns, tool use, planning — the design patterns that make "agentic" different from "chatbot," mapped back onto my own two-instance Claude workflow.

W4
Pillar 2 capstone

Vendor-neutral synthesis — the same patterns outside any one company's framing, so the understanding isn't tied to a single vendor's terminology.

W5
Pillar 6: evals — the artifact week

This is the week built to produce the strongest differentiator: a real, working eval against RouteToServe's own OCR pipeline.

W6
Pillar 4 (start) + Pillar 5 + wrap-up

Get business-translation and deployment-awareness moving, then close the sprint honestly: do I now know what I don't know, and roughly where everything lives?

The Discipline

The same rules that keep RTS shipping, applied to learning

One course at a time, ever

Nothing new starts until the current thing produces its artifact. Multiple directions at once is how self-directed learning quietly turns into nothing finished.

Artifacts over certificates

Free coursework counts for something only when it produces something buildable and linkable. "Completed a course" proves nothing on its own; "built this with it" does.

A daily gate, not just a schedule

Every session opens with a short Python read-back — reading code I didn't write until I can explain it line by line — before any new course material. The goal isn't writing fluency yet; it's never taking an AI agent's word for what its code does.

Why It's Structured This Way

The market read behind the plan

This isn't guesswork about what matters. AI orchestration — directing, interpreting, and quality-checking AI output — is named directly as the core future-of-work skill in 2026 industry research. Most employers in this space aren't hiring AI engineers from scratch; they want people who can direct AI effectively, evaluate its output critically, and apply it to real business problems. That's precisely the profile this plan builds toward — with the deep-engineering, infrastructure-heavy tier kept as an honest stretch goal, not the operating plan.

The moat isn't the certificate. It's the judgment and the proof — the same principle RouteToServe's case study makes about shipped code, applied here to how the skills behind it get built in the first place.

If You Want to Build Your Own

The actual replicable steps

None of this required permission, a budget, or someone else's curriculum. If you're trying to close a real skill gap on your own, this is roughly the order that worked:

1 — Name the gap precisely, not generically

"I should learn AI" isn't a gap. "I can direct AI well but can't explain why it did what it did, unprompted, to a stakeholder" is a gap — specific enough to source real material against.

2 — Split the destination from the plan

Write down the aspirational north star honestly, then write down the real near-term target separately. Let the north star keep ambition honest without hijacking next week's schedule.

3 — Break the gap into pillars, and check what you already have

Most gaps aren't six new things — they're two or three genuine gaps sitting next to skills already being practiced somewhere, just not named or formalized yet.

4 — Sequence a short orientation pass before a long depth build

A time-boxed sprint that maps the whole territory beats either "read everything" or "pick one thing and hope it's the right one." Depth decisions get better once you actually know the map.

5 — Anchor technical learning against something real you already own

A real codebase, a real dataset, a real project — anything with actual stakes teaches faster and produces something linkable, unlike a generic tutorial dataset nobody will ever look at again.

6 — Build one small daily gate, not just a calendar block

A short, consistent accountability habit — even 15 minutes — does more than a big weekly time block that's easy to skip when the week gets busy.

7 — Let artifacts prove it, not completion

"I finished a course" convinces nobody, including yourself six months later. Something built, shipped, or shown does the actual convincing.
